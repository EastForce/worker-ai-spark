# Making the “Human-Centered” Principle in AI Substantive

## The Goal–Consequence Mismatch in Labor Relations from a Workers’ Standpoint

**Author: Project Initiator**

**Abstract:** The “human-centered” principle is widely invoked in artificial intelligence ethics and governance, yet an abstract conception of humanity cannot determine who may define technological purposes when authority over goals and deployment is separated from exposure to consequences. Drawing on Marx’s accounts of concrete individuals, the capitalist application of machinery, and human emancipation, this article argues that ownership and control of technological conditions, the pressures of capital accumulation, surplus-value appropriation, and workers’ dependence on the wage-labor relation allow a goal–consequence mismatch in labor relations to be reproduced. A workers’ standpoint rests on situated knowledge of labor processes, exposure to technological risks, and political standing to participate in collective decision-making as co-producers, without assuming that workers are infallible. The article develops power-sensitive alignment across three levels: goal legitimation, behavioral constraints in operation, and the allocation of corrective powers at the institutional level. This framework constitutes a minimum condition for preventing AI from reinforcing labor domination, not a substitute for transforming ownership, distribution, and collective power. A workers’ standpoint gains universal emancipatory significance only when it opposes monopolization of productive conditions and unilateral domination while incorporating social reproduction, ecological sustainability, and the free development of all. The article concludes by specifying the implications of this general theory for the Worker AI Spark Project: workers’ knowledge, exposure to risk, and corrective capacity should run through its principles, preservation of labor knowledge, public evaluation, and governance research, while propositions that have not yet been practically validated, along with their boundaries and counterexamples, remain public and revisable.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence; relations of production; workers’ standpoint; goal–consequence mismatch; human emancipation

*Version note: This is a public discussion draft of the general paper in the Worker AI Spark Project’s foundational theory. It belongs to the “literature research–theory formulation” stage. It may be cited, criticized, and revised, but it has not been validated through project practice or formal peer review, and it does not represent an established consensus among workers, trade unions, experts, or institutions. ## 1. Introduction

“Human-centeredness” has become an important principle in AI ethics and governance. Relevant Chinese ethical norms emphasize advancing human well-being, fairness and justice, privacy and security, as well as rights to be informed, to choose, and to withdraw.[1] Research on human-centered AI similarly emphasizes expanding human capabilities while maintaining meaningful and actionable human control.[2] Yet when authority to set goals, deploy systems, and command corrective resources belongs to one party, while risks to income, safety, and employment fall mainly on another, the abstract claim that a system serves “humanity” does not tell us whose knowledge and interests have actually entered its objectives. In this article, the “human-centered” principle denotes a higher-order norm of AI governance, while “human-centered AI” denotes the corresponding paradigm of design and governance. Neither is equivalent to anthropocentrism in the relation between humanity and nature, and neither can substitute for the political commitment to a people-centered approach.

Existing Marxist scholarship has criticized the abstract character of human-centered AI ethics and reasserted the value-creating role of living labor in the age of AI.[3][4] Li Ruiqi and Ding Xinye, drawing on Marx’s critique of technology, examine the macro-level logic through which AI enters modes of production, mechanisms of surplus value, and structures of power. Xu Weixuan analyzes the comprehensive determination of the proletariat in the digital age through its class basis, forms, relations, and representation.[5][6] Research on algorithmic management, value alignment, and participatory AI has, from different directions, exposed problems of technical control, goal selection, and the participation of affected people.[7][8][9][10][11][12][13][14] These studies provide necessary resources. Within the literature reviewed for this article, however, relations of production, goal encoding, the visibility of knowledge, the distribution of consequences, and institutional correction have not yet been sufficiently connected through a middle-range mechanism. The concept of a goal–consequence mismatch is introduced to explain why this separation may be reproduced over time and transmitted across different AI systems. It neither repeats a macro-level account of technology and power nor substitutes for the definition of the digital proletariat as a subject.

Labor relations concentrate this problem. Research and institutional materials have documented applications of AI in recruitment, scheduling, performance evaluation, task allocation, and labor surveillance, as well as the possibility of its involvement in disciplinary and dismissal decisions.[7][8][9] This article also considers generative systems that may be used to interpret workplace rules, handle labor disputes, or offer advice about action. Algorithmic management systems and generative assistance systems are not the same technical form, but both may enter the encoding of labor objectives, the distribution of consequences, or institutional correction. The article calls the condition in which actors able to encode, deploy, and modify AI objectives are separated from those who bear most system errors, transition costs, and consequences of domination—while the latter lack effective capacity to influence objectives and correct the system—a “mismatch between goal setters and consequence bearers,” abbreviated here as the “goal–consequence mismatch.” The object of analysis is not a particular chatbot but the sociotechnical chain of power formed when AI enters labor relations.

The article advances two conclusions that must not be conflated. First, its normative claim is that, where AI reorganizes production, employment, labor management, and the distribution of technological gains, a workers’ standpoint is a necessary criterion for determining whether “human-centeredness” has substantive content. Second, power-sensitive alignment is only a minimum governance condition for preventing technology from further entrenching labor domination. It is neither workers’ emancipation nor a substitute for changes in ownership, the distribution of gains, or collective power. The argument proceeds from the roots of the mismatch in relations of production, through the three grounds of a workers’ standpoint and the mismatch’s proximate mechanisms, to minimum governance constraints. It then addresses major objections and competing explanations and identifies the demands that the theory places on the Worker AI Spark Project, together with the conditions under which it should be revised.

## 2. From the Capitalist Application of Machinery to the Form of Production Relations Assumed by AI

The explanatory power of Marxism for AI does not lie in searching classical texts for ready-made technical rules. It lies in revealing the social form taken by the productive force of machinery. This article follows a textual sequence from “concrete individuals,” through the “capitalist application of machinery,” to “free time and human emancipation.” It begins from individuals situated in social relations, explains how technology is shaped by ownership, capital accumulation, and labor control, and evaluates technical progress by asking whether reductions in necessary labor time are converted into the free development of all.

### 2.1 From Abstract Humanity to Concrete Individuals

In *The German Ideology*, Marx and Engels analyze history from the point of departure of “concrete individuals,” their activities, and their material conditions of life.[15] (p. 519) The *Theses on Feuerbach* further states that the human essence is not an abstraction inherent in an isolated individual but, in its reality, the ensemble of social relations.[16] (p. 501) These propositions do not directly grant moral priority to any group. They do, however, determine the analytical starting point of human-centeredness: human needs, choices, and capabilities must be understood within the conditions of production and life.

In concrete relations such as platform work, the same statement—“you may refuse an unreasonable demand”—has radically different practical content for a person with adequate protection, collective support, and alternative employment than for someone dependent on that day’s orders and vulnerable to account suspension.[7][8] Formal consent may be given under threat to livelihood; formal exit may mean an immediate loss of income; and a written avenue of appeal may fail because evidence is unequally held or retaliation is feared. Abstract preference satisfaction, a formal catalog of rights, and a person’s actual capabilities must therefore not be treated as equivalents. Whether AI “helps people” depends not only on the logical correctness of its output but also on whether the person receiving advice can safely understand, reject, negotiate, appeal, and act collectively.

### 2.2 The Productive Potential of Machinery and Its Capitalist Application

Marx’s analysis of machinery contains a decisive distinction. As a productive force, machinery can relieve burdensome labor, increase social wealth, and shorten necessary labor time. Its application within capitalist production, however, also serves the production of relative surplus value, labor discipline, and capital’s control over the labor process.[17] (pp. 463, 487, 493, 508) The problem can neither be blamed on machinery “itself” nor obscured by invoking the productive contribution of technical progress while ignoring its specific social form.

AI continues this duality. As a technical objectification of social knowledge and past labor, it can supplement workers’ capacities, lower barriers to specialized knowledge, and reduce dangerous or repetitive labor. Under particular institutional conditions, it may also fragment skills, concentrate judgment, extend surveillance, and reduce workers to the execution of system instructions.[7][8][9] Extending Marx’s account of machinery, when models and their infrastructure enter capitalist production and function as continuously used means of labor, they combine with managerial power and organizational knowledge.[17] (pp. 378, 393) Technical effects are therefore not determined by capability alone. They depend jointly on the relations of ownership, production, and distribution into which a technology enters.

Accordingly, this article does not revisit whether machines create value. It examines how technological objectives, productivity gains, and the adverse consequences of errors are allocated among different actors after AI enters the capital–labor relation.[4]

### 2.3 Why the Capital–Labor Relation Is Not an Ordinary Stakeholder Conflict

Asymmetry in AI-mediated labor relations cannot be explained merely by saying that managers possess more information and workers are relatively disadvantaged. The relation between capital and labor is first founded on separation from the conditions of production: one party owns or controls the material and technical conditions needed to organize production, while the other mainly obtains the means of subsistence by selling labor power. In the *Critique of the Gotha Programme*, Marx rejects the proposition that labor is the sole source of all wealth while also observing that a person who lacks productive conditions other than labor power depends for work and survival on those who possess the objects and means of labor.[18] (p. 429) This structural dependence precedes the design of any particular system and provides the material basis for determining who can write objectives into it.

Second, the technical choices of an individual firm are shaped by accumulation and competitive pressure. Even without malicious intent, demands to reduce costs, increase calculability, and strengthen process control may make labor substitution, risk transfer, and intensified surveillance reproducible directions of deployment.[7][8][9] Third, productivity gains from AI do not automatically become common welfare. Under existing relations of appropriation, they may appear as concentrated profits, rents, and powers of control, while costs of adjustment, unemployment, and retraining fall mainly on workers and public institutions. Finally, the wage-labor relation makes workers more than “affected persons.” They are active subjects who sustain production, supply data, and realize technological value, yet they ordinarily lack authority over objectives proportionate to their contribution.

Labor relations are therefore not a conflict among resource-symmetric stakeholder preferences. They connect ownership, organizational authority, the distribution of surplus, and dependence for subsistence, giving the separation among goal setters, technical controllers, and consequence bearers a structure capable of reproducing itself. A power-sensitive theory of AI must begin from that structure, rather than simply placing capital and labor beside one another as two “stakeholders” whose preferences await balancing.

## 3. The Three Grounds of a Workers’ Standpoint and the Boundaries of Its Universality

### 3.1 Structural Class Position and Concrete Position within Relations of Power

If “workers” are defined merely as the party with fewer resources in a particular decision, the term cannot carry a Marxist class analysis. If the category is confined to formal employment contracts, it excludes platform work, economically dependent self-employment, unemployed people, and labor involved in social reproduction. The article therefore distinguishes two related levels of analysis.

Structural class position concerns the relation of an individual or group to the conditions of production, the commodification of labor power, the organization of labor, and the appropriation of surplus value. What workers share structurally is neither low income nor a uniform subjective identity. It is that they participate in social production and obtain the means of life mainly through their own capacity to labor, while lacking ownership of the key conditions needed to organize production autonomously. Unemployed and prospective workers may remain within this relation even when not currently employed. Highly skilled employees may occupy a workers’ position because they do not control technological conditions. Frontline managers may occupy a contradictory position, combining dependence on wages with the performance of capital’s control functions.

The “workers’ standpoint” used here does not replace the concept of the proletariat with a looser category of workers, nor does it assign everyone affected by technology to the same class. The proletariat denotes a structural class relation in which people are separated from the conditions of production and obtain the means of subsistence mainly by selling labor power. A workers’ standpoint is an analytical standpoint that, within a concrete AI deployment, examines knowledge of the labor process, exposure to consequences, and standing to participate in collective decisions on the basis of that structural relation. The former explains why asymmetries of power repeatedly reproduce themselves; the latter explains how that structure enters particular technological decisions.

Concrete position within relations of power concerns control over objectives, information, bargaining capacity, exit, exposure to risk, and access to remedy in a particular AI decision. Structural class position shapes concrete power but does not mechanically determine every situation. Workers may acquire substantial bargaining capacity through unions, law, or scarce skills, while management may be dependent on a platform in a particular technical supply relationship. Power-sensitive alignment principally examines concrete power positions, but an explanation of why their asymmetry repeatedly appears must return to structural class relations. A single category of “vulnerable identity” cannot do the work of both levels.

Unpaid care and other activities of social reproduction require separate treatment. They may not enter the wage-labor relation, yet they sustain the reproduction of labor power and social life. This article neither forces all unpaid caregivers into the category of employed workers nor places them outside the scope of human-centeredness. It treats labor in social reproduction as an important boundary test of whether a workers’ standpoint can transcend narrow productivism.

### 3.2 Epistemic Grounds, Normative Concern, and Political Standing

A workers’ standpoint has at least three grounds, none of which can substitute for another. The first is epistemic. Through practical activity, workers possess situated knowledge of bodily strain, tacit coordination, equipment defects, the gaming of indicators, conflicts with care responsibilities, and risks of retaliation—matters that formal processes and managerial data often fail to capture. Such knowledge can correct the deployer’s tendency to equate written rules with actual operations. The article draws on standpoint theory’s discussion of situated knowledge and carries it into the labor context: making visible the position from which knowledge is produced can increase reflexivity; it does not declare one position to possess the whole truth.[19][20]

The second ground is normative. People exposed to risks to income, safety, privacy, and occupational prospects are entitled to protection of their basic rights, and potentially irreversible harms should receive attention proportionate to the exposure. This ground does not depend on workers possessing unique knowledge. Even if workers cannot accurately explain a model’s mechanism, a system may not unilaterally shift major safety risks onto them. Normative priority for those exposed is also not a pass granting priority to any demand: it remains constrained by facts, the rights of others, nondiscrimination, and public safety.

The final ground is political standing. Marx observes that the social productive power generated by combined labor appears, within capitalist organization, as the productive power of capital itself.[17] (p. 487) On this basis, the article argues that the cooperation and organizational knowledge on which AI systems depend are not created solely by those who control technological conditions. Worker participation in technical objectives is therefore not an accidental intervention by an external “stakeholder” in someone else’s property. It is a demand by co-producers to obtain control over common conditions that govern their own activity. Standing to participate follows from the demand to be free from unilateral domination and to organize social activity jointly; it cannot be canceled on the ground that experts possess greater technical knowledge. Epistemic contribution can explain why participation improves judgment, normative concern can explain why protection is required, and political standing explains why workers retain the right to propose goals, register dissent, and trigger review even when experts disagree.

The three grounds jointly constitute a workers’ standpoint without idealizing actual workers. Workers may be mistaken about facts. They may advance claims that harm other workers, consumers, or the environment. Conflicts among workers also arise from differences in employment form, skill, gender, region, and degree of organization. A genuine workers’ standpoint must therefore preserve disagreement, accept correction by evidence, and prevent the least audible workers from being replaced once more by a small number of representatives.

### 3.3 From a Particular Class Position to the Conditions of Universal Emancipation

A workers’ standpoint does not gain universality merely because workers are numerous, disadvantaged, or possess distinctive knowledge. Marx’s early writing states that the proletariat claims no special right and cannot emancipate itself without emancipating other spheres of society.[21] (p. 17) On this basis, the universality of a workers’ standpoint is reconstructed here as a demand for self-transcendence. If workers’ emancipation means only that one group of producers receives a larger share within existing relations, it remains a group interest. It acquires universal emancipatory potential only when it aims to abolish the relation in which a minority monopolizes the conditions of production and the majority is compelled to sell labor power and submit to unilateral domination. This reconstruction does not treat suffering itself as a source of truth or turn early texts directly into contemporary rules for AI governance.

This also requires a strict distinction among minimum rights protection, political participation, and human emancipation. In *On the Jewish Question*, Marx explains that political emancipation is important progress but is not equivalent to human emancipation, in which concrete individuals reorganize separated social powers as their own common powers.[22] (pp. 32, 46) Likewise, rights to information and appeal, worker consultation, and safer model output are worth pursuing, but even their full realization would not automatically change ownership of AI infrastructure, appropriation of productivity gains, or dependence on wages. The governance framework proposed here has only defensive and transitional significance. It can reduce harm, enlarge agency, and create conditions for collective change; it cannot substitute technical participation for social transformation.

The universality of a workers’ standpoint also consists in its ultimate orientation toward overcoming the worker identity, rather than preserving compelled labor indefinitely. AI’s potential to reduce necessary labor should be converted into free time and the development of capacities for everyone, rather than into nonworking time for a minority alone. The *Communist Manifesto*’s account of the free development of each as the condition for the free development of all[23] (p. 53), together with Marx’s writing on disposable time,[24] (pp. 197–203) provides the normative measure for this judgment. Technical progress should therefore be assessed not only by output and numbers of jobs, but also by authority over working time, the social distribution of productivity gains, and whether people must still accept arbitrary domination as a condition for obtaining the means of life.

This universality always has boundaries. Labor is not the sole source of material wealth; nature is also a source of use values.[18] (p. 429) The interests of children, disabled people, unemployed people, caregivers, and future generations cannot wait for recognition until they become wage workers. A workers’ standpoint can avoid degenerating into producer-centrism only by joining social reproduction, ecological sustainability, and equal dignity for all, and by refusing to transfer costs to other disadvantaged groups.

The literature on “pro-worker AI” distinguishes five directions of technical change: labor augmenting, capital augmenting, automating, expertise leveling, and creating new tasks.[25] The issue here is not to predict which direction must occur, but to show that the selection of a technical direction is itself constrained by authority over goals, the distribution of gains, and the capacity for collective action.

## 4. The Goal–Consequence Mismatch: Conceptual Boundaries, Structural Sources, and Mechanisms of Transmission

The goal–consequence mismatch does not mean that an objective is illegitimate merely because the goal setter and consequence bearer are different persons. It describes an institutional disconnection between control over objectives and exposure to consequences: actors able to encode, deploy, or modify system objectives do not bear a proportionate share of the errors and transition costs that follow, while those who bear most of these consequences find neither their situated knowledge nor their risks adequately included in the objective and lack effective powers to challenge evidence, secure review, or change the system. The distribution of gains and the severity, accumulation, and irreversibility of harm are dimensions for measuring the extent of the mismatch, not conditions that must all be present before the concept applies. Risk externalization is only one manifestation of the mismatch at the level of outcomes. The concept links goal formation, the visibility of knowledge, the distribution of consequences, and institutional correction, while using the capital–labor relation to explain how such disconnection may reproduce itself.

### 4.1 From Behavioral Alignment to Goal Legitimation

AI alignment commonly asks whether system behavior conforms to instructions, intentions, preferences, interests, or values. Gabriel notes that these objects differ from one another and that selecting an alignment target is both a normative and a political problem.[10] Global ethical guidelines converge around fairness, transparency, responsibility, and the avoidance of harm, yet differ substantially in interpretation and implementation.[11][12] Recent Chinese scholarship has also questioned the limitations of treating abstract “human values” as a single input, approaching the problem through value symbiosis, reverse value alignment, and human–AI relations.[26][27][28]

This article does not redescribe political conflicts among human actors as “horizontal alignment.” The boundary of alignment should be preserved. Human–machine alignment addresses whether a machine’s behavior reliably follows a legitimate goal; relations among human actors raise the separate questions of how a goal becomes legitimate, how power is allocated, and how harm is distributed. Power-sensitive alignment means that the former technical problem must be constrained by the latter social and political problem. The more accurately and efficiently a system can execute an objective, the more necessary it is first to justify that objective. Otherwise, technical capability may amplify the consequences of an illegitimate goal.

The contribution of the present framework, relative to adjacent theories, is not another general principle requiring “attention to vulnerable people.” Conventional value alignment identifies the relation between model behavior and instructions, preferences, or values, but may assume that people with greater capacity to articulate preferences and deploy systems can speak for “human preferences.” Algorithmic fairness reveals differences in group metrics and the distribution of outcomes, but may become detached from relations of production, dependence on wage income, reversibility of harm, and capacity to appeal.[29] Participatory AI and worker-centered design bring affected people into design and auditing, but do not necessarily address ownership and control of technological conditions, appropriation of surplus value, or retaliation risks within participation.[13][14][30][31][32] The present account does not replace those approaches. It places goal encoding, the visibility of knowledge, risk externalization, and asymmetry in correction within one chain of mechanisms. If that chain explains nothing beyond what existing approaches already explain, there is no need to establish “power-sensitive alignment” as a distinct concept.

### 4.2 Structural Sources of the Mismatch and Four Proximate Mechanisms

The more remote sources of the goal–consequence mismatch are ownership and control of technological conditions, accumulation and competition, appropriation of productivity gains, and workers’ dependence on the wage-labor relation. These conditions shape who has the resources to propose development requirements, purchase systems, and modify models, as well as who can shift the costs of experimentation onto others. Under these structural conditions, the mismatch may enter concrete systems through four proximate mechanisms.

The first is a goal-encoding mechanism. The party that pays development costs and decides procurement and deployment can more readily translate its objectives of efficiency, cost reduction, and control into “user requirements,” while workers’ silence under pressure to maintain a livelihood may be misread as consent. The second is a knowledge-visibility mechanism. Formal workflows, quantified performance, and managerial data enter a system more readily than tacit coordination, bodily strain, conflicts with care responsibilities, and risks of retaliation. The third is a risk-externalization mechanism. Organizations may obtain gains from deployment while workers and public institutions bear erroneous account suspensions, wage loss, safety incidents, devaluation of skills, and burdens of appeal. The fourth is an asymmetry-of-correction mechanism. The party controlling logs, interfaces, and contractual authority can modify a system, while workers may not know the rules by which they are evaluated or may lack a channel capable of suspending and reversing an erroneous decision. These four mechanisms are theoretical candidates and remain open to empirical testing.

Together, the four mechanisms form a testable chain. Relations of ownership and competition shape organizational goals; organizational goals affect which forms of knowledge become data and indicators; technical operations then allocate gains and expose different actors to adverse consequences; and existing relations of power determine who can correct an error. A system that formally “treats everyone the same” may still generate structural bias because it takes inequalities that precede the system as evidence of shared values and voluntary choice. Power sensitivity does not require a system to presume that workers are correct in every dispute. It refuses to treat existing power of control simultaneously as a factual advantage and as proof of legitimacy.

### 4.3 A Unified Object of Analysis: The Chain of Power after AI Enters Labor Relations

Algorithmic management, generative advice, and institutional governance may belong neither to one technical system nor to one lifecycle. Recruitment, scheduling, and performance systems deployed by a firm encode organizational goals as classifications and decisions. Assistance systems used by workers may interpret and challenge those rules, but may also reproduce them by treating managerial data as settled facts. Systems used in arbitration, inspection, or other governance processes may correct earlier decisions or reproduce them again. The owners and controllers, direct users, and operating environments of these three kinds of systems may be entirely different.

They can be analyzed together not because they form one product but because they intervene in a shared chain of power within labor relations: who may define the problem, which knowledge becomes computable evidence, who bears the consequences of a decision, and who can cause that decision to be corrected. The goal–consequence mismatch can travel across systems along this chain. An opaque evaluation produced by an employer’s system may become a settled fact for an assistant used by a worker and later an evidentiary input for a governance system. Conversely, independent worker tools and public governance systems may interrupt this transmission.

A theoretical framework should therefore examine objectives, operation, and institutions separately along the same chain of power, rather than treat them as stages of one system. The objective level concerns goal legitimation. The operational level subjects classifications, recommendations, decisions, and actions to behavioral constraints. The institutional level allocates corrective powers for cross-system auditing, objection, review, and suspension of deployment. This maintains a coherent object of research while recognizing organizational boundaries among different AI systems.

## 5. Power-Sensitive Alignment: A Minimum Governance Structure and Its Practical Limits

### 5.1 Objective, Operational, and Institutional Levels

Power-sensitive alignment does not assign a new moral personality to machines. It imposes minimum governance constraints on the chain of “objective–operation–correction” created after AI enters labor relations. It consists of goal legitimation at the objective level, behavioral constraints at the operational level, and the allocation of corrective powers at the institutional level. These levels address different objects and need not belong to the same product or organization, but together they determine whether the people who bear technological consequences can challenge and change them.

The objective level examines ownership and control of technological conditions, the purpose of deployment, evaluative indicators, and arrangements for distributing gains. Its core question is not whether one more abstract ethical principle has been added, but who can define the problem, who benefits from deployment, and who bears the costs of experimentation and transition. “Efficiency” in automated scheduling cannot be represented only by rates of order completion; it must include the predictability of working time, travel among locations, rest, and conflicts with care responsibilities. The “accuracy” of a performance model cannot be separated from whether harm is reversible or who bears wage loss during an appeal. A high-risk use must demonstrate necessity. Formal consent given under dependence on wage income cannot establish the legitimacy of an objective, and aggregate benefits cannot conceal major harms concentrated on workers.

The operational level concerns classifications, predictions, recommendations, decisions, and automated actions. It requires verifiable facts, a floor of basic rights, identifiable risks and uncertainties, meaningful options for workers to refuse or exit, and the possibility of review and reversal for consequential decisions. Generative AI must also distinguish known facts, information requiring verification, legal judgments, and value positions; it must not present resignation, public complaint, or litigation as costless options. The institutional level concerns data use, participation, auditing, objection, and version revision. Workers and their collective representatives should be able to enter the definition of uses and indicators, learn how their views were handled, preserve dissent, trigger independent review, and suspend deployment where risks to income, health, dismissal, or privacy are high. A one-time satisfaction survey, a choice among preferences within a predetermined design, or the invitation of only cooperative representatives does not prove that power has shifted.[13][14][32]

The three levels cannot substitute for one another. A legitimate objective executed unreliably causes concrete errors and harm. Reliable operation in pursuit of an illegitimate objective may make labor domination more efficient. Inadequate corrective power leaves objectives and operations subject to the deployer’s unilateral interpretation. Power-sensitive alignment matters because it subjects technical reliability to the legitimacy of objectives and subjects both to institutional correction that workers can actually use.

### 5.2 A Governance Framework Cannot Substitute for Emancipatory Politics

Power-sensitive alignment does not replace alleged value neutrality with the assumption that workers are unconditionally correct. It requires objectives, evidence, and the distribution of risks to be openly justified. Systems must still verify facts and protect the rights of other workers, consumers, and third parties. Complex social production requires coordination, safety, and quality control. Yet the technical necessity of coordination does not automatically establish the legitimacy of unilateral command by capital, profit maximization, or appropriation of surplus value. The intensity of participation and review should rise with risks to income, health, employment, and privacy.

In the high-risk labor deployments considered here, legal safeguards, substantive participation, and reliable technology are generally necessary but insufficient. They cannot by themselves change ownership of AI infrastructure, the distribution of technological gains, capital accumulation, or the commodification of labor power. Nor can AI substitute for trade unions, collective bargaining, labor inspection, social protection, or public policy. The three-level framework is therefore only a minimum defense and a transitional arrangement against technology’s reinforcement of labor domination. If used to declare existing power relations legitimate or to substitute individualized advice for collective power, it becomes technological solutionism.

### 5.3 Institutional Translation in Chinese Labor Governance

In China, this minimum governance structure cannot be reduced to firm-level “stakeholder participation.” It should be embedded in two distinct institutional paths: algorithmic management within firms, involving platform rules, scheduling, evaluation, reward, and discipline; and public labor governance, involving employment impact assessment, employment guidance, labor inspection, mediation, and arbitration.[33][34] Neither path can be evaluated by efficiency gains alone. The former must prevent firms from shifting costs and risks unilaterally to workers. The latter must prevent administrative classification, erroneous data, and evidentiary rules from creating new forms of procedural domination. Public infrastructure, union participation, collective bargaining, and social protection also provide institutional conditions for changing control over objectives and the distribution of consequences that differ from unilateral firm deployment. Relevant policy documents show that efficiency and rights, and application and risk, have entered the agenda together; they cannot be treated as evidence that this article’s theoretical propositions have already been established.

A critique of the capitalist application of machinery should not be applied without distinction to every deployment relation in a socialist market economy. Public technological infrastructure, labor policy, collective bargaining, union participation, and public-service AI may constrain unilateral firm objectives. Employment impact assessment may include public employment and social protection within the objective; collective bargaining may alter algorithmic rules; and public systems may socialize evidentiary and enforcement costs otherwise borne by individuals. Yet public ownership or government deployment does not automatically eliminate monopolies of information or procedural domination. These institutional possibilities can materially alter control over objectives and the distribution of consequences only when they become capacities for information, participation, review, and gain sharing that workers can actually exercise.

Power-sensitive alignment raises three questions for these institutional practices. Does employment impact assessment examine who bears risk rather than calculate only net changes in jobs? Do intelligent rights-enforcement and arbitration systems allow workers to check facts, understand reasons, and trigger human review? Can platform consultation influence algorithmic objectives and the next system version rather than merely explain established rules? International Labour Organization case studies of social dialogue on AI and algorithmic management, and Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development research on worker consultation, offer empirical material for comparing forms of participation.[35][36]

This article only distinguishes conceptually between a people-centered political position and the design paradigm of human-centered AI; it does not develop a complete theory of the former. Within its limits, the conclusion is narrower: workers cannot be treated merely as abstract “users.” They participate in social production and technical operation, bear the consequences of errors, and share standing to judge the legitimacy of rules. “Human-centered” AI becomes more than the deployer’s moral description of its own objectives only when that status takes material form in capacities for goal legitimation, behavioral constraint, and institutional correction.

## 6. Major Objections, Competing Explanations, and Conditions of Applicability

### 6.1 Does a Workers’ Standpoint Sacrifice the Universality of Human-Centeredness to a Particular Interest?

A direct objection holds that assessing AI from a workers’ standpoint replaces universal human interests with the interests of a class or group, potentially ignoring consumers, managers, people outside employment, and future generations. The objection identifies a genuine danger, but it does not justify a return to abstract “humanity.” Treating a workers’ standpoint as a necessary criterion does not mean that any worker’s view is naturally correct, or that labor status should decide every domain exclusively. It reflects the identifiable structural asymmetry among technological control, contribution of knowledge, exposure to risk, and dependence for subsistence within labor relations. Refusing to analyze this asymmetry does not produce neutrality. It merely treats existing authority over objectives as the default representative of universal interests.

Whether a workers’ standpoint acquires universal significance depends on its capacity for self-limitation and self-transcendence. It must protect basic rights of other workers and third parties, recognize differences among workers, accept factual verification and public refutation, incorporate social reproduction, ecological boundaries, children, disabled people, unemployed people, and future generations, and ultimately aim to reduce compelled labor and unilateral domination. A proposal advanced in the name of workers cannot derive legitimacy from the label alone if it transfers risk to more vulnerable people, excludes those unable to speak, or places the preservation of one group’s existing jobs above the safety and freedom of all.

### 6.2 Is the Goal–Consequence Mismatch Merely an Information-Asymmetry or Principal–Agent Problem?

A competing explanation argues that information asymmetry, organizational failure, principal–agent relations, or imperfect risk management can fully explain the phenomena described here, making an account in terms of relations of production unnecessary. General organization theory can indeed explain some proximate phenomena: managers may be unable to observe the actual labor process, suppliers and deployers may have divergent goals, or diffused responsibility may leave errors uncorrected. This article does not exclude these explanations. For the goal–consequence mismatch to possess independent explanatory force, it must further explain why similar informational problems reliably make some objectives easier to resource, some forms of knowledge less visible, some actors more likely to bear errors, and corrective powers more likely to accompany control of technological conditions and contractual authority.

The capital–labor relation is not offered as a single cause determining every concrete action. It is a structural explanation of why a particular distribution may repeatedly reproduce itself. If control of productive and technical conditions, dependence on wages for subsistence, and appropriation of productivity gains predict the distribution of goal encoding, risk externalization, and corrective capacity no better than organizational size, technical complexity, or individual cognitive bias, the production-relations explanation should be narrowed. Conversely, if changes in ownership and control produce systematic differences in workers’ knowledge entering objectives, gain sharing, and rights of review even when model capabilities are similar, that would provide an empirical direction supporting the proposed mechanism. The present text states a researchable theoretical expectation, not a completed causal demonstration.

### 6.3 Are Participation, Open Source, and Correct Output Already Sufficient?

A third objection holds that questions of power become unnecessary once a model produces accurate and fair answers, or once a project opens its source code and invites worker participation. This confuses technical attributes, participatory procedures, and practical powers. Visible code does not mean that workers can understand, run, or modify a model, nor that training data, compute, deployment interfaces, and authority over versions are open. A consultation does not mean that workers can set the agenda, preserve dissent, trigger review, or suspend a high-risk deployment. Correct output in an individual case does not establish the legitimacy of a deployment objective or the distribution of gains. Open source, participation, and accuracy may all be important, but none substitutes for another, and their formal existence does not prove that power has shifted.

The same boundary constrains a project that identifies itself with a workers’ standpoint. A project cannot prove that it represents workers by its name, license, public repository, or sympathetic language alone. It cannot describe polite responses, case submissions, or limited participation by particular workers as broad consensus. Avoiding the transformation of a workers’ standpoint into another moral label requires a concrete account of which materials are authorized, which claims derive from public literature, which mechanisms remain untested, and who can register dissent and initiate revision.

### 6.4 Empirical Boundaries and Conditions for Revision

The article principally explains power relations formed when AI enters production, employment, labor management, labor knowledge, and dispute resolution. In medical diagnosis, intimate relations, military decision-making, or consumer systems not principally structured by labor relations, a workers’ standpoint may remain relevant but cannot become the sole analytical criterion without additional argument. Even within labor, public deployment, cooperative ownership, strongly unionized environments, scarce-skill labor, and nonwaged activities of social reproduction may alter the direction and intensity of the proposed mechanisms. They cannot be covered without qualification by a single model of firm-based employment.

The four proximate mechanisms remain subject to empirical testing. The explanatory scope of the goal–consequence mismatch and power-sensitive alignment should be narrowed, reconstructed, or abandoned if differences in power across labor settings do not reliably affect the inclusion of workers’ knowledge, the transfer of deployment risk, or the capacity to correct errors; if participation with agenda-setting and review rights produces neither more distinctive information nor stronger corrective capacity than downstream consultation; if public, cooperative, or collective forms of ownership do not alter the distribution of objectives and consequences; or if another theory explains the same phenomena with fewer assumptions. Future studies may compare algorithmic management, worker participation, and the distribution of productivity gains under different conditions of ownership and governance. Until such studies are completed, candidate mechanisms must not be presented as facts already validated by the project.

## 7. Theoretical Requirements for the Worker AI Spark Project

### 7.1 The Project’s Object of Analysis Is a Sociotechnical Chain of Power, Not an Isolated Model

As the general paper of the foundational theory, this article first defines what the Worker AI Spark Project should study. The concern is not simply whether a model gives “pro-worker” answers. It is how problem definition, knowledge collection, model or rule formation, deployment and use, exposure to consequences, and institutional correction are connected. An independent tool may help workers locally while remaining constrained by employer data, legal institutions, and technical infrastructure. A public system may reduce an individual’s evidentiary burden yet create new procedural domination through erroneous data and unappealable classification. The project’s principles, preservation of knowledge, evaluation, and governance research should therefore be traceable to one chain of power. Linguistic sympathy cannot be equated with a material standpoint.

### 7.2 A Workers’ Standpoint Imposes Epistemic, Normative, and Governance Constraints on the Project

Epistemically, the project should preserve situated knowledge of labor processes while recording the nature of its source, evidentiary status, scope of authorization, and possible dispute. Workers must never be asked to invent, combine, or dramatize experiences to fit a predetermined conclusion. Normatively, risks to income, safety, privacy, employment, and retaliation should operate as constraints not easily offset by aggregate benefits; consumers, third parties, caregivers, and ecological limits must also be recognized. In governance, workers should not appear only as research subjects or data providers. The theory should leave practical channels for them to formulate questions, refuse publication, preserve dissent, correct records, and influence rules. These are requirements that the theory places on the project, not factual claims about the project’s present capacity or representativeness.

### 7.3 Public Evaluation Can Test Only Limited Claims; It Cannot Replace Theory or Political Judgment

Evaluation may examine whether a system recognizes asymmetries of power, distinguishes facts from value judgments, identifies risks, respects minimum rights, and provides a reviewable path. It cannot use an aggregate score to prove that a model “represents workers,” nor can it prove that ownership, the distribution of gains, or collective power has changed. Evaluation questions, standards, and reasons for scores may reproduce biases in the visibility of knowledge, and must therefore disclose their sources, scope, counterexamples, and versions. The significance of open code and public standards lies in making criticism and replication possible, not in granting the project correctness in advance.

### 7.4 Foundational Theory Must Make Its Incompleteness Public

This article provides a common starting point for subsequent thematic papers without preempting their treatment of workers’ status as subjects, rights over cases and experience, minimum rights, system design, impact assessment, open-source governance, evaluation validity, theoretical boundaries, and chains of responsibility. Later papers may revise concepts introduced here, expose conflicts between the general theory and a specific field, or show that a mechanism applies only under limited conditions. The theoretical framework should preserve versions, reasons for revision, and unresolved disagreements so that the “literature–theory” process can be examined. If project practice is undertaken in the future, observed discrepancies should become new evidence rather than facts selectively displayed to decorate an established theory.

This openness does not mean that all claims have equal value. The project may maintain normative commitments against unilateral domination, for basic rights, and for the free development of all, while requiring empirical statements to be verified, causal explanations to face competing theories, and institutional proposals to face counterexamples and consequence testing. Truth-seeking here does not mean claiming possession of truth. It means making even the project’s most central standpoint open to public challenge and preserving the possibility of revision in light of better facts and arguments.

## Conclusion

When AI enters the labor process, it does more than add another tool. In particular relations of deployment, it combines social knowledge, capitalized means of labor, and organizational power, enabling technical objectives to enter recruitment, scheduling, evaluation, discipline, and dispute resolution at greater scale. When technological conditions and authority over deployment are concentrated while workers bear most risks to income, safety, and employment, a system may deepen the separation between goal setters and consequence bearers even if it follows instructions accurately. Labor relations therefore become a crucial field for testing whether human-centered AI possesses substantive content.

A workers’ standpoint does not replace abstract “humanity” with another abstract identity. It contains three independent demands: recognition of workers’ situated knowledge, priority in preventing their exposure to irreversible harm, and political standing to participate in collective decisions and correct systems. Power-sensitive alignment gives these demands form as goal legitimation at the objective level, behavioral constraints at the operational level, and the allocation of corrective powers at the institutional level. It can provide a minimum defense, but it cannot substitute for changes in ownership, the distribution of gains, working time, or collective power.

The article’s two conclusions must therefore remain distinct. Where AI reorganizes production, labor, and distribution, excluding a workers’ standpoint makes a complete claim of human-centeredness impossible. Including that standpoint, however, satisfies only a necessary test; it proves neither that workers have been emancipated nor that a sufficient program of human emancipation has been established. A workers’ standpoint gains universal emancipatory potential only when it moves beyond a demand for one group’s share within existing relations, aims at overcoming monopolization of productive conditions and unilateral domination within wage labor, and joins care, ecology, and the free development of all. Its theoretical value does not lie in assigning a class identity to machines. It lies in compelling AI governance to answer continuously: who may define the objective, who bears the cost of error, and who has the power to correct the system?

For the Worker AI Spark Project, that conclusion operates both as an external critical standard and as a form of self-constraint. The project cannot exempt itself from source verification, rights boundaries, public objections, or analysis of power merely because it describes itself as grounded in a workers’ standpoint. Nor can it claim that the goal–consequence mismatch has been overcome merely because code is open or an evaluation framework exists. Foundational theory becomes a basis for shared inquiry, refutation, and revision rather than a certificate of correctness only when unresolved questions are made as public as the theories already proposed.
