# How Can Workers Become Subjects of Artificial Intelligence Governance?

## The Normative Configuration of Rights, Capabilities, Voice, and Remedy

**Author:** Project Initiator[^version]

**Abstract:** As artificial intelligence enters recruitment, scheduling, monitoring, performance assessment, remuneration, and discipline, it may develop productive forces, but it may also encode existing managerial power into opaque and extensible technical rules. An abstract commitment to being "human-centered" does not by itself enable workers to shape rules or correct errors. Drawing on Marx's analysis of machinery, the labor process, and free human development, and engaging the capability, non-domination, and workplace-democracy traditions, this paper reconstructs workers' standing as governance subjects in concrete AI deployments as four institutional functions: rights, capabilities, voice, and remedy. These functions respond respectively to the trading away of status, the failure to convert formal entitlements into action, the unilateral formation of rules, and the inability to correct errors. Where AI continuously or substantially allocates important opportunities and initial consent or exit cannot control the resulting ongoing power, the four functions form a set of candidate baseline conditions that remains open to criticism and testing. They offer a normative framework for asking whether a deployment recognizes workers as subjects; they are neither an exhaustive catalog of legal rights nor evidence that control over production, the distribution of technological gains, or workers' organizational power has already changed.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence; labor governance; workers as governance subjects; labor process; non-domination; worker standpoint

## I. Statement of the Problem

Artificial intelligence is expanding from a tool that assists production into a means of governing the conditions of labor. Data-driven systems may generate, execute, or materially support recruitment screening, task allocation, dynamic scheduling, performance scoring, remuneration calculations, behavioral monitoring, and disciplinary action. These systems can increase the speed of information processing and improve some forms of prediction and coordination. Once objectives, metrics, data, and models enter managerial processes, however, they can also allocate workers' income, time, health, skills, and occupational opportunities at greater scale and frequency. Research on algorithmic management shows that technology can reinforce labor control under some institutional arrangements and expand worker autonomy under others; it should therefore be presumed neither inherently oppressive nor inherently emancipatory.[^1]

Marx's analysis of machinery reveals the tension between technology's productive potential and its capitalist application. Machinery can shorten necessary labor time, expand free time, and promote the development of human capacities. Under capitalist relations of production, however, it confronts living labor in the social form of capital and becomes a means of reorganizing the labor process, intensifying control, and pursuing surplus value.[^2] The character of AI-mediated labor governance cannot therefore be determined by technical performance alone. It must be examined within social relations: who sets objectives, who controls the conditions of production, who bears transition risks, and who can correct errors. Model accuracy does not establish the legitimacy of deployment; the presence of system bias does not predetermine a single institutional response.

Existing AI ethics frameworks repeatedly affirm safety, fairness, privacy, well-being, autonomy, and human control.[^3] These principles are indispensable, but an abstract category of "the human" can conceal concrete positions of power in employment relations. Even if a system raises average productivity, accuracy, or majority satisfaction, this does not show that workers can shape technological objectives, exercise rights in practice, influence continuously updated rules, or secure the correction of errors. Conversely, a worker-centered approach does not give workers an ultimate veto over every technical matter. Professional responsibilities, organizational coordination, public safety, internal minorities, and third-party rights must also enter the judgment.

Recent research has analyzed the production logic and capitalist application of AI through objectified digital-intelligent labor, labor-value theory, technological revolution, and Marx's theory of machinery.[^4] This paper advances a narrower question: when AI continuously or substantially affects access to work, tasks, evaluation, remuneration, discipline, skills, or conditions of exit, what institutional functions are minimally required for workers to be treated as governance subjects?

The paper proceeds through normative conceptual reconstruction. First, it limits the object of evaluation to AI deployments and accompanying labor institutions that continuously or substantially allocate important opportunities to workers. Second, beginning with the higher-order criteria of equal standing and freedom from arbitrary domination, it asks what institutional tasks arise at the stages of recognizing status, enabling action, forming rules, and correcting errors, rather than generating a classification from four predetermined labels. Third, it compares how labor rights, the capability approach, non-domination, workplace democracy, participatory AI, and effective remedy cover these tasks. Finally, it uses objections based on legitimate management, genuine consent, external regulation, internal majorities, and absorption by ownership to calibrate the framework's scope. The contrastive scenarios used below are not empirical cases. Existing cases and experiments illustrate the possibility of mechanisms or institutional feasibility; they do not establish universal causal effects for the four functions.

Marxism and neighboring theories perform different roles in this method. Marx's analysis of machinery and the labor process identifies the object of inquiry and its power relations, orienting evaluation toward whether workers can participate in mastering socialized conditions of production. Capability, non-domination, workplace-democracy, and effective-remedy theories supply mid-level institutional concepts. These concepts are included only insofar as they reveal how AI redistributes purpose, control, risk, and correction within the labor process; a liberal procedural checklist cannot substitute for analyzing relations of production. "Workers' standing as governance subjects" in this paper specifically denotes their standing in a concrete AI deployment. It is not equivalent to the full realization of workers as a class subject or historical subject.

## II. The Historical-Materialist Foundation of AI-Mediated Labor Governance

### 1. From a Technical Artifact to a Sociotechnical Relation

An AI deployment in labor, as used here, is an organization's use of a system with inferential, predictive, generative, ranking, or recommendation functions to generate, execute, or materially support rules and decisions affecting workers' important opportunities. The object of evaluation is not an isolated model but a deployment relation jointly constituted by the technical system and the labor regime. It includes the model, data, interface, organizational rules, employment contracts, collective relations, rights-enabling resources, and dispute mechanisms. The same model can have different normative qualities when situated within different ownership structures, labor institutions, and organizational relations.

Historical materialism requires technology to be understood through the interaction of productive forces and relations of production. Technological advance can transform instruments of labor, forms of cooperation, and social productive capacity. The purposes technology serves and the distribution of its benefits and risks, however, depend on concrete relations of production. AI is neither an autonomous historical subject detached from human beings nor a purely neutral tool. It encodes existing objectives and power into data structures, performance metrics, access permissions, and automated processes; through ongoing updates, it then reshapes the labor process. Evaluating AI requires neither a rejection of technology nor an identification of productive development with human emancipation. It requires asking whether workers can participate in mastering and transforming socialized conditions of production.

Marx's "free laborer" can dispose of their own labor-power while, because they do not possess the necessary conditions of production, being compelled to sell that labor-power.[^5] This combination of formal freedom and material dependence has not disappeared in digitalized work. If "voluntary" exit entails losing one's principal income, formal choice alone cannot establish substantive freedom.

Rights, capabilities, voice, and remedy are not a catalog extracted directly from Marx's writings. They are a mid-level institutional reconstruction of the foregoing analysis of production relations. Capital's control over the conditions and process of production may treat workers' bodies, time, and livelihoods as inputs to valorization; rights are therefore needed to demarcate standing that cannot be traded away at will. Separation from the conditions of production may deprive formal freedom of the conditions needed for action; capabilities are therefore required. When the objectives, metrics, and technical conditions of associated labor are determined unilaterally, producers struggle to influence the order they collectively sustain; voice is therefore required. When socialized technological power confronts workers as an independent power and concrete decisions inevitably admit error, remedy is required so that adverse decisions can be contested and corrected. The four are mid-level tasks involved in workers' control over concrete conditions of production, not a sufficient definition of labor emancipation.

AI also gives existing power new technical forms. Processing at scale may rapidly propagate local error; performance metrics, feedback loops, and opaque models may raise the cost of contestation; path dependence after procurement, expansion of use, and version updates may quickly make one-time consent obsolete; and the separation of vendor, deployer, and employing entity may fragment responsibility. These are not facts that necessarily occur in every deployment. They are mechanisms suggested by research on algorithmic management, lifecycle-governance frameworks, and existing regulation, and they remain to be tested.[^6] They indicate that AI governance must extend across procurement, use, updating, and withdrawal. A one-off review or end-stage appeal cannot substitute for continuing constraints.

These institutional tasks are not unique to AI. AI is distinctive insofar as it may change their objects, timing, and chains of responsibility. Rights may need to encompass data provenance, inferential uses, version changes, and multi-party obligations. Capabilities may need to encompass human override, alternative processes, and the capacity to work after system withdrawal. Voice may need to move upstream into procurement, metrics, training data, and version governance. Remedy may require decision logs, traceable responsibility, rollback, and vendor cooperation. Where a deployment creates none of these new problems, the four functions should be understood as applications of general principles of labor governance, not proclaimed as a uniquely AI-specific normative theory.

### 2. Why Workers Have Distinct Standing as Subjects

Workers cannot be treated merely as ordinary stakeholders for three reasons. First, equal personhood and basic rights mean that no worker may be treated as a sacrificable means simply because average benefits increase. Second, workers are situated within the concrete labor process and may identify workloads, informal cooperation, implementation gaps, and circumvention that managerial data and model developers cannot readily observe. This situated knowledge can reveal problems, but it does not make workers' views inherently correct.[^7] Third, workers are continuously governed by the rules and jointly sustain the technical system; they therefore have reason to demand participation in forming rules that affect their important opportunities. Their status as governed persons is the primary ground, while their status as co-producers is a reinforcing ground.

This standing gives workers' views no automatic epistemic or moral priority. Differences among workers, minority rights, and third-party interests remain subject to public justification. Worker-centered governance does not unconditionally transfer power from one group to another. It requires that the ongoing power to allocate working conditions not depend solely on one side's goodwill.

A basic judgment follows: power that affects important human choices must be publicly justified and may not leave those subject to it under arbitrary domination. In republican theory, domination is not identical to every interference. It is one party's capacity to alter another's choices arbitrarily and without effective control.[^8] This judgment does not exclude necessary organizational coordination, professional judgment, or public regulation.

### 3. From Standing as a Subject to Four Failures of Governance

Standing as a subject must be converted into institutional powers if it is not to remain an abstract declaration. AI-mediated labor governance can generate four failures of subjecthood. First, workers' privacy, health, equality, and livelihoods may be treated as ordinary costs offset by aggregate benefits: a **status failure**. Second, rules may formally provide consent, refusal, exit, or appeal, while inadequate information, the costs of exercise, dependence, and retaliation make those entitlements unusable: a **conversion failure**. Third, workers may be informed or consulted without being able to exert influence proportionate to risk before objectives, uses, metrics, and conditions of deployment are fixed: a **rule-formation failure**. Fourth, after error or injury occurs, the original decision-maker may monopolize evidence and review so that objection cannot produce suspension, reversal, or repair: a **correction failure**.

Rights, capabilities, voice, and remedy respond respectively to these failures. They describe different institutional relations: rights recognize justified claims; capabilities supply the practical conditions for exercising them; voice creates ex ante and ongoing influence; and remedy enables ex post correction. Bringing them into one analysis prevents written rights from standing in for the conditions of exercise, consultation from standing in for actual influence over rules, and a service portal from standing in for a correction mechanism empowered to change an outcome. Transparency, explanation, human oversight, data control, and collective bargaining should be assigned to the function they actually perform. If a necessary task cannot fit within any of the four, the classification must be expanded.

## III. Four Institutional Functions of Workers' Standing as Governance Subjects

### 1. Rights: Recognizing Status That Cannot Be Traded Away at Will

The rights function asks what a worker may claim from whom and what status or interests may not be offset at will by general efficiency. In AI-mediated labor governance, relevant rights may concern non-discrimination and occupational health and safety; privacy and limits on data use; floors for working conditions and remuneration; notice of consequential decisions and their principal grounds; organization and collective action; refusal of manifestly improper uses; and substantive review by a human decision-maker. Where model vendor, procurer, deployer, and employing entity are separate, duties of disclosure, prevention, evidence production, consultation, review, and compensation must also be allocated. Each party cannot escape responsibility by saying that it was not the final decision-maker.

The point of rights is not the number of listed items but whether workers can use them to require identifiable actors to perform duties and to constrain ordinary efficiency calculations. General productivity, satisfaction, training investment, or participation counts cannot offset unjustified severe discrimination, major health hazards, unlawful surveillance, or irreversible losses of livelihood. The independent basis of the rights function is equal standing. If privacy, health, equality, and basic livelihood can be traded away solely for aggregate gains, training, consultation, or appeal merely executes a rule that already permits the weak to be sacrificed. This claims priority for basic rights over ordinary benefits; it does not turn every labor interest into an absolute right.

This paper defines only the normative function that rights must perform. It does not propose an exhaustive set of rights valid across jurisdictions. The content of specific rights, duty bearers, and non-derogable floors requires separate rights theory and jurisdiction-specific research. Rights without the conditions of exercise or influence over rules remain insufficient to realize workers' standing as governance subjects.

### 2. Capabilities: Converting Formal Standing into Real Action

The capability function requires workers to be able to convert rights, information, and resources into valuable action. The capability approach shows that people with identical resources or formal liberties may possess different substantive freedoms because of differing personal, social, and environmental conversion conditions.[^9] Within employment relations, a person "can refuse," "can exit," or "can appeal" in a meaningful sense only when they understand the relevant information, can bear the costs of acting, and will not face unreasonable dismissal, loss of pay, suspension, stigma, or retaliation for exercising that option.

Capability cannot be reduced to individual digital literacy. At the individual level, workers need to understand a system's purpose and limitations, form independent judgments, and safely override its conclusions when necessary. At the organizational level, capabilities may require relevant logs, paid time, learning opportunities, non-automated alternatives, and safety redundancy. They also include alternative ways of maintaining work when the system fails or is withdrawn, preventing the organization from attributing its own automation dependence to individual workers.

Capabilities also have a collective dimension. Even a worker who understands a model may lack the resources needed to obtain logs, retain expert assistance, bear stoppage costs, or resist retaliation. An organization that provides individual training but no shared ability to verify data, participate during paid time, or obtain external support converts an institutional responsibility into an individual duty to adapt. Collective and public enabling conditions may include expert support, data access, anti-retaliation measures, resources for collective bargaining, and affordable conditions of transition.

The capability function has an independent rationale because standing as a subject must be convertible into action. Written rights, seats at meetings, and appeal portals cannot give workers control over power affecting important opportunities when information, dependence, costs, or retaliation make them unusable. Capability may be supplied by individual, collective, or public resources; it does not require every worker to possess every resource alone.

### 3. Voice: Producing Actual Influence Before and During Rule Formation

The voice function requires workers to influence the formation of rules. Workers may participate directly; act through authorized and accountable collective representatives; or use an external institution that workers can trigger and that must give reasons. Through these channels, experience, evidence, and objection must enter decisions and exert actual influence over problem definition, intended use, metrics, deployment conditions, and continuing revision. Participatory AI research warns that information provision, symbolic consultation, and actual power must not be conflated.[^10] A survey, focus group, or co-design workshop may elicit knowledge. If the deployer still controls the agenda, alternatives, budget, and final decision, workers remain sources of information rather than co-authors of the rules.

Voice should move upstream, before procurement, development, and deployment plans are fixed, and should allow workers to propose non-deployment and non-technical alternatives. Workers also need the information, time, and independent assistance necessary to understand disputes. Representatives must be authorized by and accountable to the workers concerned, while organizations must explain how workers' views were handled. Relevant case studies and experiments indicate that, under some conditions, social dialogue and collective power can affect AI adoption and regulation. Their results are limited by geography, industry, and research design and should not be generalized without qualification.[^11]

The intensity of voice should vary with risk. For an assistive tool with limited, reversible effects that can be discontinued without cost, advance notice, information checking, ongoing feedback, and an account of the response may suffice. A deployment that continuously affects income, health, discipline, access to work, skills, or conditions of exit may require negotiation or co-decision. Experts' epistemic advantage about technical facts cannot by itself determine who bears risk, and a worker majority cannot cancel the basic rights of a minority.

The voice function follows from the need of governed persons to control continuing power, not from the inherent correctness of workers' views. Workers need channels of influence proportionate to risk where the effect is substantial or ongoing, initial authorization cannot cover evolving uses, exit would impose unreasonable livelihood loss, and external institutions cannot absorb workers' reasons and change general rules before or during deployment. Where genuine consent, low-dependence exit, or strong ex ante public regulation already controls the power effectively, the form and intensity of voice may be reduced.

### 4. Remedy: Making Adverse Decisions Correctable

The remedy function requires that, when workers' important rights or interests are adversely affected, they can obtain timely, relatively independent, enforceable correction at an affordable cost and through a process capable of changing the outcome. Remedy is neither a customer-service entrance nor an explanatory review in which the original decision chain merely repeats its own score. Remedy proportionate to risk includes at least three stages. Workers must be able to learn of the decision and its principal basis and obtain necessary records. Where harm may be irreversible, they must be able to object, suspend implementation, and initiate review not controlled by the interests of the original decision-maker. Once an error is established, income, position, eligibility, or reputation must be restorable, and repeated errors must feed back into process, model, and version governance. Existing labor policy and platform-work regulation provide some institutional forms of notice, explanation, human review, and dispute resolution. Their existence does not show that a complete remedial chain operates effectively across jurisdictions.[^12]

Independence need not correspond to one fixed institution. What matters is that the reviewer is not controlled by the original decision-maker's interests and has authority to change the outcome. Review may uphold the original decision, but it cannot merely be self-confirmation by the original decision chain.

Remedy can correct a failure after it occurs; it cannot replace rights floors, real capacities to act, or voice during rule formation. Conversely, ex ante joint governance cannot guarantee that data, models, and implementation will never err, so it cannot eliminate independent correction. Recurring errors should trigger changes to rules or versions. Remedy has an independent rationale because technical systems and organizational decisions are fallible. When a consequential decision may be irreversible and the original decision-maker controls the evidence, power is controlled only if objection can trigger review, alter the outcome, and repair harm.

### 5. Rules for Combining the Four Functions

The four functions address distinct failures of subjecthood. Their baseline content and characteristic formal substitutes are summarized in Table 1.

**Table 1. Four Functions of Workers' Standing as Governance Subjects in AI-Mediated Labor Governance**

| Failure of subjecthood | Institutional function | Central question | Baseline content | Formalistic substitute |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Status is traded away | Rights | What must not be done, and who owes what duty? | Basic rights, identifiable duty bearers, and enforceable limits | Principles are comprehensive, but ordinary gains may still offset rights |
| Standing cannot become action | Capabilities | Can formal entitlements be used in reality? | Information, time, skills, alternatives, anti-retaliation protection, and collective resources | An exit button exists, but using it means losing one's livelihood |
| Rules are formed unilaterally | Voice | Can governed persons influence rules before deployment and throughout governance? | Entry before procurement, proposal of alternatives, reasons for handling views, and authority proportionate to risk | Workers attend co-design, but objectives and decisions do not change |
| Errors cannot be corrected | Remedy | Can a consequential adverse decision be changed and repaired? | Records, suspension, independent review, reversal, restoration, and system-level correction | An appeal portal exists, but nobody has authority to alter the outcome |

*Source: Synthesized by the author from the relevant theoretical and institutional materials.*

Four rules govern the combination of these functions. First, each function must have baseline content proportionate to risk and usable in practice; symbolic surveys, paper buttons, and portals through which nobody can change the outcome do not satisfy this requirement. Second, severe violations of basic rights, and the absence of effective remedy for consequential adverse decisions or serious errors, cannot be offset by productivity, satisfaction, training, or participation counts. Third, institutional demands should rise with severity, duration, irreversibility, scale, dependence on the work, and information asymmetry, and should rise further where alternatives, exit, and contestation are weak. Fourth, jurisdictions, organizations, and forms of ownership may adopt different institutional forms, but a power that the stronger party can withdraw without reason is not a reliable safeguard.

Risk classification changes how intensively capability and voice are realized; it does not eliminate their minimum functions. Minimum capability means workers can understand and use relevant powers without retaliation. Minimum voice means workers' reasons enter a process empowered to change general rules. If genuine exit or strong ex ante public regulation already performs the latter function reliably, direct worker participation may fall to zero, but the institutional function of absorbing governed persons' reasons cannot be zero.

Within this candidate framework, the four functions should not simply be weighted into an overall certification score. Four contrastive scenarios illustrate their non-substitutability. Allowing a majority to exchange a minority's privacy and safety for bonuses shows that participation cannot replace a rights floor. Removing human judgment and alternative processes shows that paper entitlements cannot replace real capabilities. Letting an employer determine procurement, objectives, and uses unilaterally shows that ex post protection cannot replace voice during rule formation. Lacking an independent channel able to change errors in scheduling, pay, or discipline shows that ex ante joint governance cannot replace remedy. These scenarios test only whether each function has an independent role. Stable substitution supports merger; an uncovered necessary task requires expansion.

## IV. Boundaries and Falsifiability of the Four Functions

### 1. Legitimate Managerial Authority, Genuine Consent, and Public Safety

Workers' continuous subjection to rules does not automatically create a right of co-decision over every technical matter. Complex production requires division of labor, coordination, timeliness, and assignment of responsibility. Physicians, engineers, safety personnel, and public institutions may bear non-transferable professional or legal duties. Legitimate managerial authority should have an identifiable source and subject-matter scope, be capable of explaining necessity and reasons, align authority with responsibility, and remain constrained by time limits, records, objection, and review. Participation and review themselves have temporal and organizational costs, and delay may affect organizational survival, other workers' employment, or patient and consumer safety. The intensity of voice must therefore be assessed proportionately.[^13]

Contract and property can provide initial reasons for limited managerial authority, but they cannot amount to a one-time authorization of unforeseeable, continuously updated algorithmic rules that substantially alter working conditions after contracting. Genuine consent requires at least disclosure of important uses and risks, refusal without unreasonable loss of livelihood, feasible alternatives, and a defined scope and duration of authorization. Where these conditions truly hold, voice requirements for low-dependence and reversible arrangements may be substantially reduced. Independent regulators, judicial review, and strong labor law may perform part of the voice function if they can absorb workers' reasons and alter general rules before deployment or during operation; review that addresses only individual harm after the fact is remedy.

Among equally effective mechanisms, the lower-cost and less intrusive form should be preferred. Ordinary managerial convenience, however, cannot offset basic rights. Emergency exceptions should be confined to serious and imminent risk, sufficient evidence, the absence of less harmful alternatives, and ex post reviewability.

### 2. Internal Difference, Representative Accountability, and Third-Party Boundaries

There is no unified "worker will" that can simply be entered into a system. Employees, highly skilled workers, contractors, platform workers, job applicants, disabled workers, and workers with caring responsibilities face different risks. Collective participation without inclusion and accountability may transform unilateral managerial domination into internal domination by a majority or representative.

Representative institutions must rest on genuine authorization and accountability. Collective decisions cannot exclude minority basic rights or individual remedy. Factual disagreements should be tested against evidence; distributive conflicts require public reasons and an account of effects on the least advantaged. Individual feedback must not be used to bypass collective organization, and collective decisions must not close individual avenues of remedy.

Workers' distinct standing cannot be asserted at the expense of other workers, carers, consumers, public safety, or ecological conditions. Scheduling, monitoring, and performance rules may affect family care and reasonable accommodation for disabled persons. The full AI lifecycle also carries environmental burdens through energy, water, materials, and hardware.[^14] These concerns set external boundaries on the present analysis. Ordinary transition costs may be fairly shared after rights floors are secured; violations of basic rights cannot be cured by compensation alone.

### 3. Absorption by Ownership and Structural Limits

Existing ownership structures may absorb the four functions. Without changing ownership of models, data, and infrastructure or control over residual gains, a firm can establish workshops, training, and appeal portals that turn worker participation into a means of extracting knowledge, dispersing responsibility, and manufacturing legitimacy. Even where all four procedural forms appear to exist, owners may retain ultimate power to close the organization, sell assets, distribute surplus, and withdraw participation.

Whether the four functions exist must therefore be judged by actual powers and by whether one side can withdraw them unilaterally. Reliable powers should be secured through law, collective agreement, co-determination rules, independent oversight, or another arrangement the deployer cannot cancel alone. A convenience temporarily granted by company policy does not by itself establish stable standing as a governance subject. If a deployment cannot provide the necessary basic rights, real conditions for exercising them, influence over rules, or remedy for consequential decisions—or if the deployer refuses necessary evidence and external review—the system should be paused, stopped, or not deployed, rather than patched with training, explanation, or compensation.

Even when all four functions meet their baseline content, ownership, distribution of gains, working time, and collective power may continue to subject workers to structural domination. The four functions can therefore indicate only whether a concrete AI deployment recognizes workers as governance subjects. They cannot independently prove transformation of the relations of production. Transparency, data control, human oversight, collective bargaining, ownership, and benefit sharing may be contents or enabling conditions of the four functions, or they may be independent demands of justice beyond the scope of this paper.

### 4. Competing Classifications, Defeating Conditions, and Research Limits

The four-part framework faces at least three competing classifications. "Effective rights" might incorporate capabilities and remedy as conditions for realizing rights. "Democratic accountability" might encompass both voice and correction. Non-domination might serve as one principle governing all four. This paper retains four functions not because they are mutually exclusive legal forms, but because they permit separate examination of valid claims, practical exercise, influence over rules, and correction of error. One institutional instrument may perform multiple tasks. If a merged classification omits no failure and loses no diagnostic information, the four-part framework has no independent necessity.

The classification accepts four kinds of defeating evidence. If another mechanism reliably performs the same normative task, the related functions should be merged. If a task essential to standing as a subject cannot fit within the four, their completeness is defeated. If, within the paper's domain, one function is entirely absent while power remains effectively controlled and workers' standing remains intact, the necessity of that function should be narrowed. If every counterexample can be absorbed simply by expanding the definition of voice or remedy, the framework fails for lack of discriminating power.

When functions conflict, analysis should first examine basic rights and irreversible harm, then compare less harmful means of achieving the same purpose, and only then balance ordinary negotiable interests. Efficiency, democracy, and expertise do not remove the burden of justification.

This paper did not conduct a systematic or scoping review under a preregistered protocol and reports no original survey, interview, experiment, or deployment data. It cannot establish the completeness of the literature behind the four-part framework, estimate the prevalence, cost, or causal effects of institutional arrangements, or determine risk thresholds valid across industries, jurisdictions, and employment relations. The cited cases and experiments demonstrate only possible mechanisms or feasible institutional forms; their geographic, sectoral, and methodological limits remain.

The four functions have not been converted into a measurement instrument tested for content validity, inter-rater agreement, or equivalence across groups. Future research should calibrate indicators with workers in different employment relations, collective organizations, managers, technical personnel, and legal professionals under conditions of genuine and authorized participation. Anomalous cases should test whether the framework should be merged, expanded, or narrowed. Worker support rates, institutional effects, or statistical fit cannot alone prove a normative proposition correct, but they may reveal omitted conditions, infeasible institutional forms, and unforeseen harms.

## V. Theoretical Implications for a Worker-Centered Open-Source AI Project

As a normative theory, the four functions constrain not only an abstract category of "other deployers" but any project that claims to conduct AI research, evaluation, or governance from a worker standpoint. For the Worker AI Spark Project, this means workers cannot be treated merely as sources of cases, data, or user feedback. If the project later handles real labor cases, constructs evaluations, or develops governance rules, it must ask what enforceable rights submitters and affected workers possess; whether they have the real conditions needed to understand, refuse, correct, and withdraw; whether their reasons can affect the rules; and whether erroneous disclosure or improper processing can be stopped, repaired, and prevented from recurring.

These are theoretical demands on the project, not claims about its current achievements. This paper does not show that the Worker AI Spark Project has established stable worker participation, representation, review, or joint-governance institutions. Nor does it turn unauthorized personal narratives into collective worker positions. At the literature-to-foundational-theory stage, the project can identify the four functions as constraints on itself, preserve objections and unresolved disagreements, and define testable interfaces for future practical research.

The division of theoretical labor also needs to remain clear. This paper asks why workers possess standing as subjects of AI governance and what four kinds of institutional function that standing requires. It does not exhaust a concrete bundle of rights; specify every rule governing knowledge contributions and case authorization; or replace separate theories of AI impact assessment, open-source governance, evaluation validity, responsibility chains, and benefit distribution. Rights, capabilities, voice, and remedy are mid-level functions here. Subsequent research may specify, differentiate, or revise them in response to counterexamples.

Open source should be evaluated through the same logic. Inspectable code, documents, or evaluation criteria can reduce some information barriers, but inspectability does not automatically create real capabilities, influence over rules, or remedy capable of changing outcomes. Open source can become one condition of workers' standing as governance subjects only when it is joined to enforceable rights, usable resources, accountable participation, and effective correction. "Worker-centered open-source AI" is therefore, in this paper, an institutional direction to be built and tested, not an accomplishment established by an open-source label.

## VI. Conclusion

AI deployment in labor both expresses the development of productive forces and encodes existing social relations into technical systems. Whether workers move from objects of governance to governance subjects in a concrete deployment cannot be determined by model performance or average outcomes alone. It requires examining how technological power affecting their important opportunities is formed, exercised, and corrected. This paper has therefore proposed rights, capabilities, voice, and remedy as four institutional functions with preliminary reasons for differentiation, and has limited their application through baseline content, rights floors, escalating risk, and the requirement of reliable practical safeguards.

Where AI continuously or substantially allocates important opportunities, and initial consent or exit cannot control the ongoing power, each function has a preliminary claim to necessity. If a critical task entirely lacks a usable institutional response proportionate to risk, workers have not yet been shown to be treated as subjects. Yet stable substitutes, independent failures left uncovered, or legitimate deployments in which a function is absent without impairing subject status may require the framework to be merged, expanded, or narrowed. Even complete realization of all four functions would not establish that control over the means of production, allocation of technological gains, or workers' organizational power had changed. A set of governance procedures cannot substitute for transformation of the relations of production.

As one thematic foundational-theory paper of the Worker AI Spark Project, this paper proposes a normative configuration open to criticism, comparison, and revision. It does not claim that the project has already realized the four functions, and it does not present the views of any participant, organization, or AI system as a worker consensus. Its theoretical value depends on retaining diagnostic power through further literature research, public criticism, and future practical research—and on being rewritten when evidence or counterexamples require it.
